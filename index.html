Unit 1

Building a pandemic-proof world

Pandemics have been among the deadliest events in history. New tools in synthetic biology and AI could make deliberate or accidental outbreaks worse than anything nature produces. Nature sets a baseline, not a ceiling.

Deaths from major pandemics · rough estimates that vary widely

Hover or tap a bar for the range.

Is nature still the worst bioterrorist? The course argues no. Natural pathogens aren't optimised to harm people, so they're a floor on what's possible. Engineered agents, and new risks like mirror life, don't have to respect that floor.

Prevention as fire safety. Fires became rare not because of braver fire-fighters but because of boring infrastructure. Building codes and smoke alarms did most of the work. The course argues that pandemics could go the same way, and I find that one of the most hopeful ideas in it.

From my group doc

My takeaways

AI and nature will co-create risk in the future. The dichotomy is false. Agency in the face of these risks is crucial.

Unit 2 · Prevent

Preventing engineered pandemics

Delay patient zero. The course splits prevention into deterring people who might cause harm and denying them the means. Denying covers everything from rules on risky research to screening DNA orders.

The four prevention levers · click one
The unilateralist's curse · drag the sliders

Imagine many groups who could each decide alone to publish a dangerous method. It only takes one of them to misjudge. The more groups there are, the more likely it is that someone goes ahead, even if each one is careful. The course's example is the horsepox synthesis paper.

The chance is 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. The fix is to make sure nobody decides alone, by building a norm of consulting others before publishing.

Unit 3 · Detect

Detecting early infections

Prevention has failed and someone is infected. The first job is noticing that something is spreading at all, which is hardest when it's new or spreads without symptoms. Then you need to work out what it is and start finding cases. Pathogen-agnostic tools like metagenomic sequencing read every fragment of DNA and RNA in a sample, so they can flag things nobody knew to look for.

Unit 4 · Withstand

Blocking transmission

Non-pharmaceutical interventions mostly work whatever the pathogen, so they can be ready in advance and used within hours. Lockdowns are blunt. The course focuses instead on protecting essential workers with good masks, and on making indoor air safer with ventilation and far-UVC light.

This is a toy version of the Wells Riley model. Risk rises with how infectious the air is and how long you stay, and falls with how much clean air you get. The room size and infectiousness are made up to show the shape, not real numbers for any pathogen. The humidity penalty for UV comes from my reading notes, which suggest UV loses much of its effect above about 60 to 70% humidity.

Stockpiles: as of 2022, the US Strategic National Stockpile had only enough N95 respirators to cover essential workers for 10 to 20 days, and many countries are in a similar or worse position.
From my group doc

What I learned

Far-UVC clearly kills airborne pathogens at safe doses, but estimates of how easily each pathogen is killed vary by orders of magnitude between studies. Humidity probably matters. Conventional UV loses much of its effect above about 60 to 70% relative humidity. We also need to be careful about sterilising the environment too much, because our immune system is dynamic and needs that stress-testing.

Unit 5 · Combat

Exiting the pandemic

Vaccines and treatments are usually specific to one pathogen, but they may be the only way out of a pandemic. Speed is everything. Some drugs and vaccines can be prepared in advance to work across whole families of viruses, and platforms like mRNA can be repurposed fast. A capable adversary might design around existing vaccines, though, which is why the physical barriers from Unit 4 still matter.

From my group doc

What I learned

mRNA vaccines weren't waiting on a discovery. The idea was proposed in 1988 and the key paper published in 2005, so what was missing was money and belief, not science. Operation Warp Speed invented nothing. It bought six candidates across three platforms, promised to pay even for the failures, and cut the review between phase 2 and phase 3 from six months to three weeks. R&D isn't the problem. We need infrastructure to scale solutions faster and more robustly.

My open questions

D'Souza says the Warp Speed model only works when no scientific breakthrough is needed, so it couldn't have produced mRNA in 2010. What pays for the thirty unfashionable years beforehand? Which platform is in that position right now, and how would we even know?

Unit 6

Contributing to biosecurity

The field is small and multidisciplinary. Many of the tools and organisations a pandemic-proof world needs only exist as drafts. The course's message is that if the role you want doesn't exist, you should build it.

From my group doc

What will have prevented catastrophic pandemics?

Moving from reacting to outbreaks to building continuous, adaptive protection. Better air filtration will have made transmission harder, and surveillance and screening will be able to recognise unfamiliar threats. I'm especially interested in borrowing from biological immunity to build defences that learn, rather than relying on lists of known dangers.

From my group doc

How my thinking changed

From pandemic response to the architecture of prevention, and towards building safety directly into AI-enabled biology. The challenge is systems that recognise emerging risks without being so restrictive that they block legitimate research.

Open questions

Biosecurity doesn't have enough people, and it's hard to weigh serious but unlikely long-term risks. It feels like we're placing bets on our future. How do we get more people into the field, and how do we create multidisciplinary environments?
